# Live caption readability research

Research checked on 5 October 2026. This note concerns presentation of live translated text; it does not establish Soniox accuracy, a measured Japanese-to-English delay, or a universal reading speed. No live provider or production session was used for the research.

## What Stage is currently doing

Stage v0.1.4 publishes every incoming translation hypothesis immediately. `captionWindow` repeatedly chooses the newest 90 Japanese or 180 English characters, and both renderers scroll to the end after text changes. Thus the beginning of the visible text can disappear, all remaining words can wrap again, and provisional words can be replaced while somebody is reading them. The physical caption window may remain stationary while the words inside it move.

This suggests two separate fixes: control which hypotheses become audience text, and control how published text advances. Changing provider region can improve network latency, but does not address either display behavior.

## Five particularly relevant findings

| Primary source | What it demonstrates | Practical implication and limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Google Translate: stabilizing live speech translation](https://research.google/blog/stabilizing-live-speech-translation-in-google-translate/), 26 January 2021 | Words arriving late in a source sentence can alter earlier words in its translation. Google used tail masking and decoder bias toward previous output. On its English-to-German TED evaluation, erasure fell from 2.1 to 0.1 words erased per final word; stable lag stayed at 4.1 seconds, and BLEU changed from 20.4 to 20.2. | Judge time until readable, stable text, not only time until the first guess. Brief holdback can be useful. The numerical result is not Japanese, Soniox, or a guarantee for a frontend-only heuristic. Decoder bias requires model control. |
| [Google: modeling and improving text stability in live captions](https://research.google/blog/modeling-and-improving-text-stability-in-live-captions/), 30 August 2023; [CHI publication](https://research.google/pubs/modeling-and-improving-text-stability-in-live-captions/) | A study with 123 participants compared raw captions, thresholding, stabilized captions, and stabilization with smoothing. Layout-preserving token alignment and smoothing improved comfort/readability and reduced distraction and fatigue. Reflow is itself a source of instability. | Keep published line breaks and positions steady. Do not automatically port its semantic-merging policy: suppressing a correction can lose a changed name, number, or negation. Its study does not establish a Japanese translation policy. |
| [Microsoft Azure captioning concepts](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/captioning-concepts), last updated 25 February 2026 | Azure explicitly distinguishes immediate partials, final utterances, and stable partials. `SpeechServiceResponse_StablePartialResultThreshold` controls how many times a word must be recognized before partial output; the example uses five. More stability reduces flicker and can add latency. | Stable-partial handling is normal production design, but Azure's flag is not a Soniox option. Final-only captions should consume final tokens incrementally rather than wait for an entire sentence to end. |
| [Karakanta et al., From Delay to Display](https://aclanthology.org/2021.mtsummit-asltrw.4.pdf), August 2021 | Compared immediate words, whole blocks, and complete scrolling lines for English-to-Italian/German/French. Displaying complete lines in two rows achieved lower, less variable reading rates with delay around four seconds; whole blocks added more delay. Simply extending block durations created excessive lag in preliminary experiments. | Prefer short lines or phrases over either character-by-character replay or waiting for full paragraphs. This is a system evaluation on those language pairs, not a Japanese reader study; four seconds is not a Stage target or service guarantee. |
| [BBC R&D WHP306](https://downloads.bbc.co.uk/rd/pubs/whp/whp-pdf-files/WHP306.pdf), September 2015 | Studied block and scrolling subtitles with 25 frequent subtitle users and a 16-person hearing control group under normal television conditions. Complaints about speed could reflect errors, latency, or subtitles arriving in bursts. The study cautions against treating a single words-per-minute limit as a complete readability rule. | Fix revisions, uneven delivery, and lost context before imposing a hard speed limit. These English television findings cannot set Japanese character-per-second limits or predict a nonnative audience's reading speed. |

## Other systems checked

- [Amazon Transcribe partial-result stabilization](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/transcribe/latest/dg/streaming-partial-results.html) limits changes to the newest words and supplies a per-item `Stable` field. Higher stabilization trades some accuracy for faster stable output. This is recognition output; it does not certify stability of a downstream translation.
- [Google Cloud STT v2](https://docs.cloud.google.com/speech-to-text/docs/reference/rest/v2/StreamingRecognitionResult), last updated 23 October 2025, exposes `stability` for interim recognition results and `isFinal` for committed results. Stability predicts revision, whereas confidence concerns correctness. A missing/zero stability value must not be treated as measured certainty. Soniox's documented token interface provides finality, not this same stability field.
- [Whisper-Streaming](https://aclanthology.org/2023.ijcnlp-demo.3.pdf), 2023, uses LocalAgreement-2: commit the common prefix of consecutive hypotheses after more audio arrives and keep the changing suffix unpublished. Reported English ASR latency was 3.3 seconds with one-second chunks on an NVIDIA A40. That is a dataset average, not an upper bound or a Japanese translation benchmark.
- [SimulStreaming](https://aclanthology.org/2025.iwslt-1.41.pdf), 2025, combines model-specific attention-based waiting for Whisper with agreement-based stabilization for downstream translation. Its English-to-Japanese evaluation is not Japanese-to-English, and its reported latency excludes computation time. These mechanisms cannot simply be enabled on an opaque provider API.
- [WhisperLive's current source](https://github.com/collabora/WhisperLive/blob/main/whisper_live/backend/base.py) separates completed segments from an incomplete trailing segment; [its client interface](https://github.com/collabora/WhisperLive/blob/main/README.md) exposes distinct partial and committed callbacks. This is useful separation of responsibilities, not proof of superior presentation readability.
- [Sokuji](https://github.com/kizuna-ai-lab/sokuji) already offers Soniox two-way translation and subtitle surfaces. A [26 July 2026 issue](https://github.com/kizuna-ai-lab/sokuji/issues/363) reports a relevant failure: compact CJK translations become hard to read when successive segments are joined with a space and no meaningful boundary. That report concerns Chinese-to-Japanese with a different provider; it does not establish the same defect in Soniox.
- [Teams](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/teams/meetings/use-live-captions-in-microsoft-teams-meetings) exposes caption line count, position, and appearance. [PowerPoint](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/powerpoint/present-with-real-time-automatic-captions-or-subtitles-in-powerpoint) supports docked captions outside the slide as well as overlays. [Zoom](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0059762) offers pinned or movable captions. These are verified product features; the public documentation does not reveal their complete stabilization algorithms.

Japanese-to-English deserves separate testing. A [2008 analysis of human simultaneous interpretation](https://aclanthology.org/L08-1490/) found greater Japanese-to-English than English-to-Japanese delay and an effect of word order. This supports the existence of a reordering challenge, not any numerical delay prediction for a modern model.

## Proposed Stage pilot behavior

These numerical values are engineering starting points for rehearsal, not standards established by the sources above.

1. Separate the raw recognition/translation stream from audience presentation. Keep `Live drafts` for immediate revisable output. The implemented `Easy to read` controller presents only provider-final text in stationary cards; it does not promote a draft based on elapsed time. A 600 ms window batches those final tokens before display; compare 400, 600, and 1000 ms with the same recorded response stream. Any future local agreement heuristic must remain labeled provisional rather than pretending to be provider-certified confidence.
2. Fix two text rows and the left edge. Pack text at word boundaries for English and suitable Japanese boundaries; prefer punctuation/clause breaks when available. Freeze the line breaks of published text. Advance by a complete line/card instead of slicing one character off the beginning and rewrapping the entire caption. Do not animate individual characters or continuously recenter growing text. Small optional transition animation must respect reduced-motion preferences.
3. Queue finalized content, not every intermediate hypothesis. Replace unpublished drafts in place. Give a short phrase roughly 1.5–3 seconds where speech permits, but measure the added display delay. A pilot budget of about 1–1.5 seconds of extra display delay is a starting point: shorten optional holds as pending content grows. Never silently claim that a slow display queue is still live. It is impossible to guarantee both bounded lag and adequate reading time for every word during arbitrarily fast sustained speech.
4. Preserve correctness when final text revises a draft, especially names, numbers, and negation. Do not discard such corrections merely to avoid movement. Prevent old queued captions from reappearing after manual clear, reconnect, or a deliberate context reset. Keep language-change boundaries explicit so one sentence does not accidentally mix translation directions.
5. Base the quiet-period reading window on actual displayed text as well as incoming activity. A translation arriving late still needs reading time; silence or an endpoint must not erase a newly published phrase. Keep the existing six-second idle behavior as a pilot choice, with a fresh window for newly displayed output. In final-only mode, an unseen draft that expired during silence must still be eligible when it becomes final. An explicit Clear intentionally discards its pending context.

A useful follow-up is session-only translated scrollback with `Back to live`. [Zoom documents up to three minutes of caption scrollback](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0063899) following its 18 May 2026 caption changes. Stage could start with a smaller local history so a viewer can recover a missed phrase without slowing the main display or stopping the microphone. Saving or sharing that history is a separate product behavior.

## How to evaluate the change

Replay identical response fixtures through both displays: continuous speech, a large final burst, Japanese-to-English reordering, revised negation/numbers, mixed Latin product names, rapid language switches, long silence, and delayed translation after silence. Measure visible word erasure, line movement, minimum exposure time, first-display latency, stable-display latency where measurable, and the display queue's age. Provider token-arrival age is not the same as end-to-end speech latency.

Then rehearse with real speakers and readers on the actual projected display. Ask which phrases were missed and whether participants could follow the slides, rather than only asking whether captions felt fast. Preserve final text in the separate meeting record; UI fixture tests establish rendering behavior, not recognition accuracy or reading comprehension.
